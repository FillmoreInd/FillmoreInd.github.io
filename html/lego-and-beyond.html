Rebrickable: build from what you already own, or buy someone else's design

The go-to hub for MOCs — fan-designed models with full instructions and parts lists.

What is Rebrickable?

Rebrickable is a site that gives you models (both official Lego sets and fan-made designs) along with the exact list of bricks needed to build them. It doesn't sell physical bricks itself — instead it's tied in with sourcing marketplaces and sub-suppliers (like BrickLink and Brick Owl) who can pull the individual parts from manufacturers and sellers and get them shipped to your region.

What makes it different

Rebrickable is built around one clever idea: tell it what sets or loose parts you already own, and it will show you every official set and fan-made MOC you could build with them — plus exactly which extra parts you'd need to finish the job. With well over 100,000 MOCs submitted by designers worldwide, it's the largest dedicated library of custom Lego-compatible designs on the web.

Many designers sell their MOCs as "Premium" downloads: a small fee gets you a polished instruction booklet and complete parts list for a model you won't find in any official Lego set. Because you're only paying for the design rather than a boxed retail product, working this way — instructions from Rebrickable, parts sourced from BrickLink, Brick Owl, or a compatible-brick store — often comes out noticeably cheaper than buying a comparable official set, especially for large or retired designs.

How people typically use it

  1. 1

    Log your collection. Add the sets and loose parts you already have.

  2. 2

    Browse buildable designs. See official sets and fan MOCs you can build right now, or almost can.

  3. 3

    Get the instructions. Free MOCs download directly; Premium MOCs are a one-time purchase for the digital plans.

  4. 4

    Source any missing parts. Rebrickable links out to marketplaces like BrickLink and Brick Owl to fill the gaps.

Good to know: Rebrickable itself sells the digital building instructions, not physical bricks. You'll still buy the actual parts from a parts marketplace — which is exactly what keeps the total cost down.
Getting started

What exactly are Lego building blocks?

Lego bricks are small, interlocking plastic pieces that click together using a simple system of studs on top and tubes underneath. That "clutch power" is what lets a handful of bricks become almost anything: a house, a spaceship, a working crane. First produced by the Danish company The Lego Group in the late 1940s and refined into the modern stud-and-tube system in 1958, the brick has barely changed since — which is exactly why a piece bought decades ago still fits a piece bought today.

Because the design has stayed so consistent, a whole ecosystem has grown up around it: places to buy individual parts, marketplaces to resell retired sets, and independent manufacturers making bricks that click together just as well, often for a lot less money. The rest of this page is a guide to that wider world.

1

One system, endless parts

Every stud is the same size, so pieces from any era — and increasingly, from other brands — can be mixed in the same build.

2

A collector's economy

Retired sets and rare parts are actively bought, sold and traded on dedicated marketplaces, much like a stamp or coin community.

3

A builder's community

Fans design their own original models — called MOCs — and share or sell the instructions for anyone to build.

Lego and beyond

Where to buy parts, sets, and compatible bricks

A mix of official-parts marketplaces and independent, Lego-compatible brick makers. All of these sites are third parties and are not affiliated with The Lego Group.

Genuine parts marketplace

Brick Owl

Brick Owl is an independent online marketplace for buying and selling genuine Lego parts, minifigures and sets. Started in 2013 by two lifelong Lego fans, it now hosts thousands of individual seller storefronts, letting you compare prices and combine orders across stores. Vendors on Brick Owl operate out of many different countries, so while the marketplace aims to serve buyers worldwide, exact availability can vary a little depending on where a seller is based and what they're able to ship internationally.

Genuine parts marketplace

BrickLink

Founded in 2000, BrickLink is the largest resale marketplace for genuine Lego, now owned by The Lego Group itself. It's known for its enormous parts catalogue, a "Wanted List" tool for tracking down every last piece for a project, and active community forums. It's a great first stop for rare or retired parts — just note that BrickLink has scaled back buying and selling privileges in a number of countries, so it's worth checking availability in your region before you start hunting.

Visit BrickLink
Compatible bricks

Webrick

Webrick sells Lego-compatible bricks and parts — unbranded pieces built to the same stud-and-tube system, often at a noticeably lower price. It's popular with builders working on large custom projects (MOCs), thanks to a handy tool that lets you upload a parts list exported from design software and get a ready-to-order match. It also carries parts from respected third-party brands, including some colours official Lego doesn't offer.

Visit Webrick
Compatible bricks

Wobrick

Wobrick is a dedicated shop for Gobricks-branded pieces, one of the better-regarded Lego-compatible brick makers. Beyond loose parts, it specialises in turning a builder's own MOC design into a ready-to-build kit — send over your parts list and it'll quote you a complete, boxed set of the exact bricks you need.

Visit Wobrick
Compatible sets

BlueBrixx

BlueBrixx is a German brand, founded in 2018, that designs and sells its own Lego-compatible building sets — everything from architecture and trains to licensed vehicle models — usually at a friendlier price than an equivalent official set. It combines original in-house designs with collaborations from established Chinese manufacturers, and even runs a number of physical retail stores across Germany.

Visit BlueBrixx
Compatible bricks

YourWobb (YWOBB)

YourWobb, also known as YWOBB, is a Shanghai-based store selling a wide range of Chinese-brand, Lego-compatible bricks, sets and MOC kits, shipped worldwide. It stocks parts from many well-known compatible-brick manufacturers side by side, and is often used by builders looking to fill out a large or unusual parts list at a lower cost than buying official Lego pieces individually.

Visit YourWobb
Community shop

Brick Me Up Scottie

Brick Me Up Scottie grew out of a fan community around set reviews and hobby builds, and has expanded into a small shop selling Lego-compatible sets, accessories and loose parts. Its collections lean into fun, curated themes — architecture, modular city buildings and more — making it a nice browse for inspiration as much as a place to shop.

A quick glossary

A few terms you'll run into constantly across these sites and forums.

Short for "My Own Creation." Any original model designed by a fan rather than released as an official Lego set. MOCs range from small vehicles to enormous, multi-thousand-piece builds, and are usually shared with full instructions and a parts list, like the ones found on Rebrickable.
"Adult Fan of Lego." The community's term for grown-up hobbyists who collect, build and trade well beyond childhood — the core audience for most of the marketplaces and compatible-brick makers on this page.
The grip strength between two connected bricks — strong enough to hold a build together, loose enough to pull apart by hand. It's the single most important quality benchmark when comparing genuine Lego to compatible alternatives.
Lego's own service for buying individual loose parts rather than a boxed set, useful for topping up a collection or sourcing pieces for a MOC. Independent marketplaces like BrickLink and Brick Owl serve a similar purpose, often with a far larger and more specialised selection.
"Studs Not On Top." A building technique where bricks are angled or turned so their studs face sideways or downward, used to create smoother, more realistic surfaces — common in detailed MOCs.
A saved list of specific parts, colours and quantities you need, supported by marketplaces like BrickLink. Sellers can match against it directly, making it much faster to complete a large or unusual parts order.
Shorthand for "The Lego Group," the Danish company that owns the Lego trademark and manufactures official sets. None of the independent sites listed on this page are operated by TLG.
